    How to use these labs

    You already keep systems running. This course gives you the reasons behind the practices, one principle at a time, by making you hit the wall each principle exists to get you past.

    Every lab follows the same sequence, and the order matters:

    1. The page. A realistic incident or request lands on you. Read it as if you were on call.
    2. Try the bigger machine first. Do the thing an experienced sysadmin would reach for: a larger SKU, more replicas, a restart. Do it for real and write down the number. The point is to see it fail with your own eyes, so the principle that follows is an answer to a problem you have, not a slide.
    3. The principle. The theory, kept short and exact. Some labs include an instrument: a small simulator you can drag to watch the mechanism move.
    4. Build it. Step-by-step work on Azure against the lab rig.
    5. Proof. A checklist of measurable results. Tick an item only when you have the evidence saved (a query result, a chart, a log line). Ticks are stored in this browser and drive the progress bar on the left.
    6. Debrief and mentor's key. Questions to answer out loud, then notes for your mentor with expected numbers, common traps and harder variations.
    Keep a lab journal

    One Markdown file per lab, in a git repository: the page, your bigger-machine attempt with its numbers, your hypothesis, what you changed, the proof (paste the KQL and a screenshot), and one paragraph on what you would do differently in production. Your mentor reviews the journal, not your memory.

    01

    The SRE contract

    What SRE is, how it relates to DevOps, and why the instincts that make a good sysadmin are necessary but not sufficient.

    Site Reliability Engineering began at Google as an answer to one question: what happens if you staff operations with software engineers and give them the authority to treat reliability as an engineering problem? The answer was a small set of mechanisms that turn reliability from a feeling into a number, and turn arguments between developers and operators into a budget they share.

    DevOps names the goals, SRE supplies the mechanisms

    DevOps is a set of cultural goals. SRE is one concrete way to meet them, with specific practices you can check for. Google's own phrasing is that SRE is a class that implements the DevOps interface.

    DevOps goalSRE mechanismLab
    Reduce organizational silosOne SLO that product, development and operations all agree to; the error budget decides release pace, not opinions02
    Accept failure as normalTargets below 100%; blameless postmortems02, 14
    Implement gradual changeCanaries, progressive rollout, automatic rollback08
    Leverage tooling and automationToil capped at 50% of an engineer's time; automate the rest09
    Measure everythingSLIs measured at the user's edge; symptom-based alerting; observability01, 03, 12

    The tenets, in one place

    • 100% is the wrong target. Users cannot tell 99.99% from 100% through their own Wi-Fi and phones, and each extra nine costs roughly ten times more. Pick the target users actually need.
    • Users define reliability. CPU and uptime are causes. What you promise and measure is the user's experience: did the request succeed, fast enough?
    • The error budget is the contract. 1 − SLO is an allowance of failure. While budget remains, ship. When it runs out, slow down and fix reliability. Nobody has to win an argument.
    • Toil is capped. Manual, repetitive, automatable work that grows with the service is limited to half the team's time. The other half goes to engineering that removes toil.
    • Alert on symptoms, page on budget burn. A page means a human must act now to protect users. Everything else is a ticket or a dashboard.
    • Change is the largest source of outages. The Google SRE book estimates about 70% of outages come from changes to a live system. Make changes small, gradual and reversible.
    • Failure is a learning input. Postmortems are blameless and end in tracked action items.
    • Plan capacity from demand, verify with load. Forecast organic growth and launches, then prove capacity with load tests, including the loss of a failure domain.

    When the bigger machine is right

    Vertical scaling works when the bottleneck is a resource that grows with the machine: CPU-bound work, memory for a cache, disk IOPS.

    It is a legitimate tool, and Lab 04 makes you prove when it helps. The skill is knowing which kind of problem you have before you pay for the bigger SKU.

    02

    The lab rig

    A small ticket-selling service called Boxoffice, built to fail in the ways real services fail. Every lab breaks it on purpose.

    Boxoffice sells concert tickets. Fans browse events, reserve seats, then check out through a payment provider. It runs on Azure Container Apps with PostgreSQL behind it, and sends traces, metrics and logs to Application Insights through OpenTelemetry. The services it depends on but does not own (payments, pricing, recommendations) are simulated by a second app, deps, whose behaviour you change at runtime.

    Knobs

    Boxoffice's behaviour is controlled by environment variables. Changing one with az containerapp update --set-env-vars creates a new revision, which is a deploy in miniature. Most labs start by turning one knob to its bad setting; your job is to find out why it hurts and what the right setting or design is.

class Overloaded(Exception):
    """We refused work on purpose. Mapped to 503 + Retry-After."""


class UpstreamFailed(Exception):
    """A dependency still failed after our retries. Mapped to 502."""


pool = psycopg2.pool.ThreadedConnectionPool(1, DB_POOL, os.environ["DATABASE_URL"])
pool_slots = threading.BoundedSemaphore(DB_POOL)


class db:
    """Borrow a pooled connection. Waiting for one is its own span, so pool
    starvation shows up in a trace instead of hiding inside request latency."""

    def __enter__(self):
        with tracer.start_as_current_span("db.pool.wait"):
            if not pool_slots.acquire(timeout=DB_WAIT_S):
                raise Overloaded("no database connection free")
        try:
            self.conn = pool.getconn()
            if DB_PREPING:
                self._ping()
        except Exception:
            pool_slots.release()
            raise
        return self.conn

    def _ping(self):
        try:
            with self.conn.cursor() as cur:
                cur.execute("SELECT 1")
        except psycopg2.Error:
            pool.putconn(self.conn, close=True)
            self.conn = pool.getconn()

    def __exit__(self, exc_type, exc, tb):
        broken = isinstance(exc, (psycopg2.OperationalError, psycopg2.InterfaceError))
        try:
            if exc_type is None:
                self.conn.commit()
            elif not broken:
                self.conn.rollback()
        finally:
            pool.putconn(self.conn, close=broken or bool(self.conn.closed))
            pool_slots.release()
        return False

inflight = 0  # only touched on the event loop, so it needs no lock


@app.middleware("http")
async def admission(request: Request, call_next):
    global inflight
    trace.get_current_span().set_attribute("app.release", RELEASE)
    # Checkout may use the whole budget; browsing is shed first.
    limit = MAX_INFLIGHT if request.url.path == "/checkout" else int(MAX_INFLIGHT * 0.75)
    if SHED and inflight >= limit:
        return JSONResponse({"error": "busy, retry later"}, status_code=503, headers={"Retry-After": "2"})
    inflight += 1
    try:
        return await call_next(request)
    finally:
        inflight -= 1

def reserve(body: dict):

        raise RuntimeError("group pricing table missing")  # the bad release in Lab 08

            r = requests.post(f"{DEPS_URL}/pay", json={"reservation_id": rid}, headers=headers, timeout=PAY_TIMEOUT_S)

    """deps: stand-ins for the services Boxoffice depends on but does not own.

  /pay    payment provider with finite capacity: when it is slow, a queue forms
  /price  pricing engine (Boxoffice v1 calls it while holding a row lock)
  /recs   recommendations, nice to have

Run exactly one replica: all state lives in memory.
"""
import asyncio
import os
import random
import uuid

if os.getenv("APPLICATIONINSIGHTS_CONNECTION_STRING"):
    from azure.monitor.opentelemetry import configure_azure_monitor
    configure_azure_monitor()

from fastapi import FastAPI, Request
from fastapi.responses import JSONResponse

app = FastAPI(title="deps")

DEFAULTS = {
    "pay_latency_ms": 150,
    "pay_fail_rate": 0.0,
    "pay_capacity": 30,  # charges the provider works on at once; the rest wait in line
    "price_latency_ms": 20,
    "recs_latency_ms": 40,
    "recs_fail_rate": 0.0,
}
faults = dict(DEFAULTS)
pay_slots = asyncio.Semaphore(DEFAULTS["pay_capacity"])
stats = {"pay_calls": 0, "charges": 0, "duplicate_charges": 0, "pay_waiting": 0}
charged = set()
by_key = {}  # Idempotency-Key -> result, or None while the first request is still running
revert_task = None


@app.post("/pay")
async def pay(body: dict, request: Request):
    stats["pay_calls"] += 1
    key = request.headers.get("Idempotency-Key")
    if key in by_key:
        if by_key[key] is None:
            return JSONResponse({"error": "a request with this key is in progress"}, status_code=409)
        return by_key[key]
    if key:
        by_key[key] = None
    stats["pay_waiting"] += 1
    async with pay_slots:
        stats["pay_waiting"] -= 1
        # A long queue makes the provider slower too (cache churn, GC, lock contention).
        # That is what lets an overload outlive whatever triggered it.
        slowdown = 1 + stats["pay_waiting"] / max(1, int(faults["pay_capacity"]))
        # Work continues even if the caller has already timed out and gone away.
        await asyncio.sleep(faults["pay_latency_ms"] * slowdown / 1000)
        if random.random() < faults["pay_fail_rate"]:
            by_key.pop(key, None)
            return JSONResponse({"error": "provider error"}, status_code=503)
    rid = body["reservation_id"]
    if rid in charged:
        stats["duplicate_charges"] += 1
    charged.add(rid)
    stats["charges"] += 1
    result = {"charge_id": str(uuid.uuid4())}
    if key:
        by_key[key] = result
    return result


@app.get("/price")
async def price(event: int, seats: int):
    await asyncio.sleep(faults["price_latency_ms"] / 1000)
    return {"cents": 4500 * seats}


@app.get("/recs")
async def recs():
    await asyncio.sleep(faults["recs_latency_ms"] / 1000)
    if random.random() < faults["recs_fail_rate"]:
        return JSONResponse({"error": "recommendations down"}, status_code=503)
    return {"items": [{"event_id": 2, "why": "fans of Arctic Lights also bought"}]}


def apply(changes):
    global pay_slots
    faults.update(changes)
    if "pay_capacity" in changes:
        pay_slots = asyncio.Semaphore(int(faults["pay_capacity"]))


async def revert_after(seconds, previous):
    await asyncio.sleep(seconds)
    apply(previous)

    """Fans buying tickets. SHAPE picks the traffic pattern; CLIENT_RETRIES and
CLIENT_BACKOFF describe how the mobile app retries a failed checkout.

Locust is a closed-loop generator: each user waits for a response before sending
the next request, so it backs off on its own when the service slows down.
Real internet traffic keeps arriving. Expect real incidents to be worse."""
import os
import random
import time

from locust import FastHttpUser, LoadTestShape, between, task

SHAPE = os.getenv("SHAPE", "trickle")
CLIENT_RETRIES = int(os.getenv("CLIENT_RETRIES", "3"))
CLIENT_BACKOFF = os.getenv("CLIENT_BACKOFF", "0") == "1"  # honour Retry-After, else back off with jitter
HOT_EVENT = 1


class Fan(FastHttpUser):
    wait_time = between(0.5, 2)

    @task(8)
    def browse(self):
        self.client.get("/events")

    @task(2)
    def buy(self):
        event = HOT_EVENT if random.random() < 0.7 else random.randint(2, 5)
        seats = 6 if random.random() < 0.02 else random.choice([1, 2, 2, 3, 4])  # ~2% group bookings
        r = self.client.post("/reserve", json={"event_id": event, "seats": seats})
        if r.status_code != 200:
            return
        rid = r.json()["reservation_id"]
        for attempt in range(1 + CLIENT_RETRIES):
            c = self.client.post("/checkout", json={"reservation_id": rid})
            if c.status_code < 500:
                break
            if CLIENT_BACKOFF:
                retry_after = c.headers.get("Retry-After")
                time.sleep(float(retry_after) if retry_after else random.uniform(0, 2 ** attempt))


class Shape(LoadTestShape):
    """(users, spawn rate) over time; returning None ends the run."""

    def tick(self):
        t = self.get_run_time()
        if SHAPE == "off":
            return None
        if SHAPE == "trickle":  # background traffic so SLO windows have data
            return (10, 5)
        if SHAPE == "steady":
            return (100, 20) if t < 900 else None
        if SHAPE == "ramp":  # +50 users a minute up to 1000
            return (min(1000, 50 * (int(t // 60) + 1)), 50) if t < 1260 else None
        if SHAPE == "onsale":  # 20x for three minutes, starting at t=120
            if t >= 600:
                return None
            return (2000, 400) if 120 <= t < 300 else (100, 100)
        if SHAPE == "brownout":  # steady load long enough to watch a recovery
            return (150, 30) if t < 1200 else None
        raise ValueError(f"unknown SHAPE {SHAPE}")

    Why it doesn't work

    Everything on that dashboard measures a cause: how busy the machine is, whether a process is alive, whether the server sent a 5xx. Users don't experience causes.     Here they disagree for a reason you will meet in real systems: Boxoffice turns a payment failure into an HTTP 200 with "status":"failed" in the body. Every tool that judges success by status code now counts a failed purchase as a success.

    Principle · Service level indicator

    An SLI is the proportion of valid events that were good, measured as close to the user as you can afford: SLI = good events ÷ valid events.

    Write the specification first, in user terms ("a checkout succeeds and confirms in under 1.5 s"), then the implementation (which log, which filter, which threshold). Many implementations can serve one specification; the spec is what you agree with product owners.

    Where to measure
    Measurement pointSeesMisses

    Edge / load balancerEvery request that reached you, even when the app was deadFailures before the edge; meaning of the response body

    Why it doesn't work

    When a request needs components A and B to both work, and they fail independently, the request succeeds with probability A × B. Availability in series always gets worse than the weakest link.     Principle · SLO, SLA, error budget

    An SLO is a target for an SLI over a window: "99.5% of valid checkouts succeed, measured over a rolling 28 days." The error budget is what the target allows to fail: (1 − SLO) × valid events.

    Why it doesn't work

    A service is a pipeline of resources: worker threads, a connection pool, the database's CPU, its locks, the pricing service. Throughput is set by the narrowest one. Buying more of a resource that isn't the bottleneck changes nothing, and some bottlenecks don't grow with hardware at all.

    Read reserve() in v1: it locks the event's row with SELECT … FOR UPDATE, then calls the pricing service, then updates the row. Every reservation for event 1 needs that one row, so they line up and go one at a time. If the lock is held for 25 ms, at most 1 ÷ 0.025 = 40 reservations per second can complete for that event, on any hardware. This is serialization, and it is the most common reason "we scaled and nothing happened".

    Principle · Find the bottleneck before you buy

    For every resource on the path, check Utilization, Saturation (work waiting for it) and Errors: the USE method. The bottleneck is the resource that is saturated first.

    • Little's law: items in the system = arrival rate × time in system, L = λW. At 300 requests/s and 9 s each, 2,700 requests are waiting somewhere. Use it to size pools and to spot where the queue is.
    • Queueing: as a resource approaches 100% utilization, waiting time grows like 1 ÷ (1 − utilization). At 90% busy, the average wait is 10× the service time; at 99%, 100×. Latency explodes long before throughput reaches its limit.
    • Serialized work (a lock, a single leader, one hot partition) caps throughput at 1 ÷ hold time. The fix is to shorten or split the critical section, not to make the machine bigger.

    Why it doesn't work

    A retry is extra load sent at exactly the moment a dependency is least able to take it. Retries at several layers multiply: three attempts in Boxoffice times four tries in the app is up to 12 calls for one purchase. And a timeout doesn't mean the work didn't happen, only that you stopped waiting. Without an idempotency key, every retry after a timeout can be a new charge.

    Principle · Retry safely, or don't retry

    Retries are for rare, independent failures. Under overload they cause the overload. Every retry policy needs a limit on how much extra load it can create.

    • Timeouts come from the dependency's measured latency (just above its normal p99.9), and fit inside an overall deadline for the user's request: attempts × timeout + backoff must be less than what the caller will wait.
    • Retry at one layer, usually the one closest to the failure. Everyone else fails fast.
    • Back off exponentially with full jitter, so retries spread out instead of arriving in synchronized waves.
    • Retry budget: retries may add at most about 10% to normal traffic. Beyond that, fail. A circuit breaker does the same job by stopping calls entirely for a while.
    • Idempotency keys make retries safe: the provider recognizes a repeated request and returns the first result instead of charging again.

    • Timeouts shorter than the caller's, derived from the dependency's normal latency. A 30-second timeout on a 40 ms service is a promise to wait forever.
    • Degrade: serve the page without recommendations, or from a short cache. A slightly worse page beats no page.
    • Liveness must not depend on dependencies. If a liveness probe fails because a dependency is slow, the platform restarts healthy processes and turns a slowdown into an outage.

    Keep the fixes from Labs 04–06 in place (RESERVE_MODE=v2, your retry policy, the bulkhead). This lab is about what's left when everything is tuned and demand is still too high.

    Try the bigger machine first

    The sysadmin move

    Let autoscaling handle it: an HTTP concurrency rule with up to 30 replicas, and the database on a larger General Purpose SKU. Run the onsale shape and record goodput (successful requests/s that completed within the latency threshold) per 10 seconds, plus cost per hour of the setup.

  --scale-rule-name http --scale-rule-type http --scale-rule-http-concurrency 30

The new replicas then open more database connections, at the peak. Goodput dips hardest right when demand is highest.

    Why it doesn't work

    Throughput counts responses; goodput counts responses that were still useful when they arrived. When a server accepts more work than it can finish, an unbounded queue forms. Once requests wait longer than the client's timeout, the server is doing work for clients who have left, and goodput falls toward zero while the server looks 100% busy. Clients that time out retry, adding load. After the spike ends, the server still has to drain the backlog.

    Autoscaling reacts to load that has already arrived, and new capacity takes minutes. It is the right tool for demand that lasts, not for a three-minute spike. And no amount of provisioning matches a spike that is, by definition, bigger than normal.

    Principle · Admission control

    Decide at the door how much work you will accept, and refuse the rest quickly and cheaply. A fast "come back in 2 seconds" keeps the work you accepted fast.

    • Limit concurrency, not rate: by Little's law, limit = throughput the bottleneck can sustain × latency you want to keep. Requests beyond it get 503 with Retry-After.
    • Prioritize: shed browsing before checkout. A fan who is paying matters more than one who is looking.
    • Drop expired work: if the caller's deadline has passed, don't start.
    • Degrade: a cached event list from 2 seconds ago is fine during an on-sale.
    • Pre-scale for known events and use autoscaling for the unknown. A virtual waiting room (queue-based load leveling) is the next step when demand is extreme.

    Proof
    Debrief
    Why limit concurrency rather than requests per second?
    Concurrency adapts to latency. If requests get slower, a rate limit still admits the same number per second and the queue grows; a concurrency limit admits fewer, automatically, because each slot is held longer.
    Isn't returning 503 on purpose bad for the SLO?
    It spends budget, yes; your SLI counts it as bad. The alternative isn't zero errors, it's timeouts for nearly everyone. Shedding spends a little budget to keep most users served.
    What does rejecting a request cost, and why does it matter?
    Accepting the connection, parsing headers, and writing a 503. If rejection is expensive (say, it needs a database lookup to decide), then under enough overload you spend all capacity rejecting. Shed as early and cheaply as possible, ideally at the edge.

    Testing finds the failures someone thought of. Production finds the rest, and a full deploy exposes every user to them at once.     Principle · Progressive delivery

    Expose a change to a small slice of real traffic, compare that slice against the old version at the same time, and widen exposure only while the comparison is clean. Roll back automatically.

    • Blast radius = share of users exposed × time until detection × time to roll back. A canary shrinks all three.
    • Compare against a baseline, not an absolute threshold. Both versions see the same traffic and the same dependency weather, so a difference belongs to the change.
    • Compare per journey. A 2% failure on reserve is invisible in the overall error rate.
    • Detection needs volume. At 5% of traffic, a rare bug may need a long bake before you have seen enough requests to judge. "Not enough data" must hold the rollout, not pass it.
    • Decouple deploy from release. Feature flags (for example Azure App Configuration) let you turn a feature off without a deploy.

# Usage: bash rig/canary.sh <baseline-revision> <canary-revision> <baseline-release> <canary-release>

    --revision-weight "$BASE=$((100 - $1))" "$CANARY=$1"
}

rollback() {
  echo "$(date +%T) ROLLBACK: $1"
  az containerapp ingress traffic set -n boxoffice -g "$RG" -o none --revision-weight "$BASE=100" "$CANARY=0"
  az containerapp revision deactivate -n boxoffice -g "$RG" --revision "$CANARY" -o none

judge() {
  az monitor app-insights query --app appi-sre -g "$RG" --analytics-query "$QUERY" -o json |
    jq -r --arg b "$BASE_REL" --arg c "$CANARY_REL" --argjson min "$MIN_N" --argjson margin "$MARGIN" '
      .tables[0].rows
      | group_by(.[1])
      | map({name: .[0][1],
             b: (map(select(.[0] == $b))[0] // [null, null, 0, 0]),
             c: (map(select(.[0] == $c))[0] // [null, null, 0, 0])})
      | (map("  \(.name): canary \(.c[3])% of \(.c[2]), baseline \(.b[3])% of \(.b[2])") | join("\n")) as $report
      | if any(.[]; .c[3] > .b[3] + $margin and .c[2] >= 20) then "ROLLBACK\n" + $report
        elif any(.[]; .c[2] < $min) then "HOLD\n" + $report
        else "PASS\n" + $report end'
}

    Why it doesn't work

    Reliability against hardware, maintenance and zone failures comes from having more than one copy, in places that don't fail together. Size doesn't help. And redundancy isn't free of impact: failover needs detection, promotion, DNS updates and client reconnection, and each of those takes time you should measure rather than assume.

    Principle · Independent copies, enough of them

    Put copies in separate failure domains (process, host, zone, region) and give the survivors enough capacity to carry the full load when one domain is gone.

    • N+k: if you need N units of capacity at peak, run N+k so that losing k still leaves N. Across Z zones, each zone must carry peak ÷ (Z − 1) on its own.
    • Static stability: survive a failure with what is already running. Don't plan on autoscaling or any control-plane action during a zone outage; those may be impaired too.
    • Correlated failure is what breaks redundancy: two replicas on one host, one config change applied to both, a single-replica dependency (like deps here) that everything shares.
    • Clients must handle failover: pooled connections to the old primary are dead. Validate before use, retry idempotent work, and expect a burst of errors measured in seconds, not zero.

    The sysadmin move

    Why it doesn't work

    Resilience properties are claims about behaviour under conditions you rarely see. Until you create those conditions, a claim is a hope. Chaos engineering creates them deliberately, under control, when people are ready to watch.

    Principle · Chaos engineering

    An experiment, not vandalism: define steady state as a measurable SLI, predict it will hold under a specific fault, inject that fault with the smallest blast radius that tests the claim, and stop immediately if the SLI breaks a preset abort condition.

    • Steady state is an output users care about (checkout success, p95), not "all pods running".
    • Vary real-world events: process death, latency, dependency loss, zone loss, clock skew, full disks.
    • Minimize blast radius: one service, one zone, a few minutes; widen only after it holds.
    • Abort conditions are written before the run, and the stop button is tested.
    • Automate and repeat: a passing experiment becomes a regression test for resilience.

    "It fails for some products"

    Principle

    Spend

    Escalation · SupportWed 15:30

    Try the usual move first

    The sysadmin move

    Grep the logs. With kubectl logs across the services and per-host dashboards, find which products fail and why. Give yourself 20 minutes.

    You will seeFifteen services' logs, no way to connect a line in one to the request in another, and aggregate graphs where 0.4% disappears. You can find that something fails; it's very hard to find what the failing requests have in common.

    Why it doesn't work

    Monitoring answers questions you predicted: is CPU high, is the error rate above X. Observability is being able to ask a new question of the system from its outputs without shipping new code. That needs two things logs alone don't give you: correlation (every span, log line and metric exemplar from one request shares a trace ID, passed between services in the traceparent header) and high-cardinality attributes (product ID, user tier, release) so you can slice by whatever turns out to matter.

    Principle · Ask new questions without new code

    Instrument once with rich, consistent context; explore from aggregate to individual request and back.

    • RED per service (rate, errors, duration) tells you where; USE per resource tells you why it's slow; traces tell you what one bad request did.
    • The workflow: SLI dashboard → service with the anomaly → traces filtered by error or latency → the attribute the bad ones share → confirm by slicing the metric by that attribute.
    • Sampling: keeping every trace is expensive. Head sampling (decide at the start) is cheap but throws away rare errors at the same rate as everything else. Tail sampling (decide when the trace is complete) can keep all errors and slow traces and a fraction of the rest, at the cost of holding spans in the collector.
    • Compute metrics before sampling. RED metrics derived from sampled traces are wrong by the sampling rate, and wrong differently for errors than for successes.

    Debrief
    Why not add product ID as a label on Prometheus metrics?
    Every distinct label value creates a new time series. Thousands of products times services times status codes can overwhelm a metrics backend. Put high-cardinality attributes on spans and logs, which store events, and keep metric labels low-cardinality.
    Head sampling at 10% keeps 10% of errors too. Why is that a problem?
    A failure that hits 1 in 1,000 requests leaves you with 1 example per 10,000 requests, often none in the window you look at. Tail sampling keeps all of them.
    What does tail sampling cost?
    The collector holds every span until the trace is complete (the decision wait), so memory grows with traffic × wait. All spans of a trace must reach the same collector instance, which complicates scaling out (load-balance by trace ID).

    • Scribe keeps the timeline with UTC timestamps. On a small team, one person holds several roles; say out loud which.

    Proof
    Debrief
    Why not fail over the database automatically too?
    Because a false positive (a network blip between regions) would promote the replica while the primary is still taking writes: two primaries, diverging data. Many teams automate traffic failover and keep the data promotion as a deliberate, fast, rehearsed human decision.
    During the outage, region B served browsing but couldn't take reservations until promotion. Is that acceptable?
    Often yes: read-only mode is a graceful degradation. Fans can see events and are told purchases resume shortly. Make it explicit in the product, not an accident.
    Where were your dashboards and alerts during the disaster?
    If they lived only in region A, they went down with it. Monitoring for DR needs to run somewhere that survives the failure it's watching.
    Mentor's key · Lab 15
    What good looks like

    Front Door marks region A unhealthy after a few failed probes and sends browsing to B within a couple of minutes. The database promotion is the slow, manual part. A good runbook has exact commands, verification after each step, and a communication step. RPO should be close to the replication lag observed before the drill.

    Traps
    • Probing /healthz, which stays green in region B even while its database is read-only. Discuss what the probe should mean.
    • Forgetting that Boxoffice in B must point at the promoted server and that the connection string may change.
    • Leaving two General Purpose servers and Front Door running after the drill.

    Brief · Venue operationsWeek 7

    Boxoffice is opening its own venue, the 18,000-capacity Fjord Arena. Every entrance has handheld gate scanners that hold a TCP connection open to a central service, Gatehouse. Gatehouse admits ticket holders, knows how many people are inside, and must never let occupancy exceed the fire-safety limit. Scanners are already built; the protocol below is fixed.

    Pilot first: one small event, internal users only, 99% SLO. If the pilot goes well, every event at the arena depends on it.

    The protocol

    TCP, ASCII. A scanner opens one connection and keeps it for the whole event, sending one command per line (terminated by \n) and reading one response line per command, in order.

    CommandResponseMeaning
    PINGPONGLiveness check from the scanner
    NODEan identifierWhich Gatehouse instance answered; unique to that instance
    GATESan integerScanner connections open right now
    COUNTan integerPeople inside right now
    ENTER <ticket>OK <count> · DUP · FULLAdmit a ticket holder. DUP if that ticket is already inside; FULL if occupancy is at capacity.
    LEAVE <ticket>OK <count> · ERR not-insideA ticket holder left. They may enter again later.
    anything elseERR <reason>The connection stays open. A line longer than 128 bytes gets ERR too-long and the connection is closed.

    Tickets are 1–64 characters from A–Z, 0–9 and -. Capacity, ports and similar settings come from configuration (default capacity 18,000).

    • Correct behaviour for every command above, including under concurrency: with occupancy at capacity − 1, two simultaneous ENTERs on different connections admit exactly one person.

    • Health and metrics on a separate HTTP port, with liveness and readiness that mean different things.
    • RED metrics per command, and structured logs that carry a connection id.

    Phase 3 · Design (written, ~1 day)

    The pilot went well. Gatehouse now runs every event at the arena and fans are its users: the SLO is 99.95%. Design a distributed Gatehouse with at least three nodes in separate failure domains. Scanners may connect to any available node.

    • GATES returns scanner connections across all available nodes.
    • NODE returns a globally unique identifier.

    Mentor's key · Final assignment
    Phase 1 · correctness traps, most common first
    1. TCP is a byte stream, not a message stream. One read can hold half a command or three. A correct server buffers until \n, handles several lines per read and answers in order. Test it by sending PI, then NG\nCOUNT\n in a second write. Treating each fixed-size read as one command is the classic failure.
    2. The connection count must drop on every exit path: clean close, client reset, protocol error, oversized line, server exception. Look for finally (or the language's equivalent) around the connection handler.
    3. Half-open connections. A scanner whose battery dies sends no FIN, so GATES stays inflated forever. Good answers use TCP keepalive or an idle timeout tied to the 15-second PING. The most valuable thing to probe in the defence.
    4. Check-then-act at capacity. if count < cap: count += 1 on a multithreaded server lets two ENTERs through at capacity − 1. Acceptable fixes: an event loop with no await between check and update, a lock, or an atomic conditional update in a store. The test must prove it, not just exist.
    5. Load-balancer probes counted as scanners. A TCP probe on the protocol port opens connections and inflates GATES. Best answers keep health on the HTTP port and say why.
    6. Input limits. The 128-byte limit enforced while reading, not after buffering megabytes; ticket charset validated; unknown commands answered without closing.
    7. Restart semantics. In-memory state means a restart sets COUNT to 0 while 16,000 people are inside, so the capacity limit is silently gone. That is a fire-safety failure. Either persist (a write-ahead log, or a database with a unique constraint on the ticket) or accept it explicitly for the pilot with a stated mitigation; either way it must be written down.
    Phase 1 · README, readiness list, handover

    Strong readiness lists include, roughly in order: durable state; graceful shutdown and draining (scanners must reconnect elsewhere); scanner authentication (mTLS or per-device keys) and rate limiting; protocol versioning; SLOs and alerts; runbooks; capacity tests; ticket validity from the ticketing system; backup and restore; a security review (ticket enumeration, replay). Red flags: an unprioritized list of "add monitoring, add tests"; no mention of the state problem; decisions written afterwards as justifications. For tooling, look for one-command tests, a linter, CI, a Dockerfile, config from the environment and a README a stranger could follow. A small coherent project beats a sprawling one.

    Phase 2 · Operate
    • SLIs measured at the server and from the load generator (the scanner's view), with health probes excluded and latency judged against a threshold, not averaged.
    • Load report. The peak is about 400 ENTER/min plus 2,400 PING/min, which any reasonable implementation handles. What matters is a named bottleneck with USE evidence and the measured headroom.
    • The 2% scanner retries come back as DUP, even when the first attempt admitted the fan. It's the Lab 05 idempotency problem again. Strong answers propose a request id, or treat DUP from the same scanner within a few seconds as success.
    • Drill (a), process kill: connections drop, scanners reconnect, and with in-memory state COUNT resets. Score honesty about that highly.
    • Drill (b), latency: scanner timeouts against the 500 ms, and whether retries made it worse.
    • Drill (c), deploy at peak: long-lived TCP connections don't move to a new revision on their own, so a traffic-weight canary barely touches existing scanners. Good answers notice and talk about draining.
    • Postmortem: use the Lab 14 rubric.
    Phase 3 · Design

    There is no single right design; reward per-command reasoning and honest trade-offs.

    CommandReasonable choiceWhy
    NODEUUIDv4 at start, or region-zone-instance-boot idNeeds no coordination. Discuss whether it should change across restarts.
    GATESAvailable and approximateEach node publishes its local count with a TTL (an etcd lease, a Redis key with expiry, or gossip); the answer is the sum of fresh reports. Under partition it undercounts; say so and expose staleness.
    COUNT, FULLConsistentCapacity is a legal limit. A linearizable store (Raft-based such as etcd, a single-primary database with synchronous standby, a strongly consistent Cosmos DB account) with an atomic conditional increment.
    DUPConsistent per ticketShard tickets by id so one leader owns each ticket, or a unique constraint in the same store as occupancy.

    Markers of a top answer:

    • Quota (escrow) counters: capacity split into per-node allotments, admitted locally and rebalanced through the consistent store. Under partition, nodes keep admitting up to their remaining quota without ever exceeding capacity.
    • The uplink failure is the real partition. If every node is in Azure and the arena's link fails, nobody gets in. Strong designs put an on-site cell at the venue, preloaded with valid tickets and a capacity quota, syncing when the link is up.
    • PACELC: they check that a cross-zone consistent write still fits the ENTER latency SLO.
    • Questions for product: which is worse, turning away a valid fan or admitting a duplicate? Is capacity a hard legal limit? Must the venue work offline, and for how long? How stale may the scanner's numbers be? Can tickets be bound to an entrance?

    Red flags: "use Kafka" or "use Kubernetes" as the replication answer; one CAP choice for the whole service; no failure scenario walked through step by step.

The strongest answers argue it isn't needed, because a good offline mode makes 99.95% in the cloud fine for fans.

    • Ecosystem and tooling: ticket validity and revocation from the ticketing system; scanner identity and fleet management; a config service; observability and SLO dashboards; incident tooling and a status page for venue staff; drain-aware deploys; a load-test harness in CI.

    Glossary

    Admission control
    Deciding at the entrance how much work to accept, and rejecting the rest quickly. Lab 07.
    Blast radius
    How many users a failure or change can affect, and for how long. Lab 08.
    Bulkhead
    A separate, limited pool of resources for one dependency or workload, so its failure can't consume everything. Lab 06.
    Burn rate
    Speed of error-budget consumption relative to an even spend over the window. Lab 03.
    Canary
    A new version given a small share of real traffic and compared against the current version. Lab 08.
    Chaos engineering
    Controlled experiments that inject real-world faults to test a hypothesis about steady-state behaviour. Lab 11.
    Circuit breaker
    A client-side switch that stops calling a failing dependency for a period, then probes before resuming. Lab 05.
    Contributing factor
    One of several conditions that together allowed an incident; replaces the idea of a single root cause. Lab 14.
    Error budget
    The amount of unreliability the SLO allows over its window: 1 − SLO. Lab 02.
    Error budget policy
    Pre-agreed consequences of spending the budget, such as a release freeze. Lab 02.
    Failure domain
    A set of components that fail together: a process, host, zone, region, or a shared configuration. Lab 10.
    Golden signals
    Latency, traffic, errors and saturation: the four signals the SRE book recommends monitoring for any service.
    Goodput
    Useful completed work per second: responses that succeeded within the time the client was willing to wait. Lab 07.
    Idempotency key
    A client-supplied identifier that lets the server recognize a repeated request and return the original result. Lab 05.
    Incident commander
    The person who coordinates an incident response, delegates and decides, and does not debug. Lab 13.
    Jitter
    Randomness added to retry delays so clients don't retry in synchronized waves. Lab 05.
    Little's law
    Items in a system = arrival rate × time in system. Lab 04.
    Load shedding
    Deliberately rejecting some work under overload to keep serving the rest. Lab 07.
    Metastable failure
    An overload that persists after its trigger is removed, sustained by a feedback loop such as retries. Lab 05.
    MTTD / MTTM / MTTR
    Mean time to detect, mitigate, repair or resolve. Useful for discussion, statistically weak as targets. Lab 13.
    N+k
    Provisioning k units beyond what peak needs, so losing k still leaves enough. Lab 10.
    Observability
    The ability to answer new questions about a system's behaviour from its telemetry, without new code. Lab 12.
    Postmortem
    A written, blameless analysis of an incident with tracked action items. Lab 14.
    RED
    Rate, errors, duration: per-service request metrics. Lab 12.
    Retry budget
    A cap on retries as a fraction of normal traffic. Lab 05.
    RPO
    Recovery point objective: maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time. Lab 15.
    RTO
    Recovery time objective: maximum acceptable time until service is restored. Lab 15.
    SLA
    Service level agreement: a contract with consequences if a level isn't met. Looser than the SLO. Lab 02.
    SLI
    Service level indicator: the proportion of valid events that were good, measured from the user's point of view. Lab 01.
    SLO
    Service level objective: a target for an SLI over a window. Lab 02.
    Static stability
    Continuing to work through a failure using only what's already running, without needing control-plane actions. Lab 10.
    Tail sampling
    Deciding whether to keep a trace after it completes, so errors and slow traces can always be kept. Lab 12.
    Toil
    Manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical work without lasting value that grows with the service. Lab 09.
    USE
    Utilization, saturation, errors: per-resource checks for finding bottlenecks. Lab 04.
